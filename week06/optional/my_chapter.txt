The Knowledge Problem
Language models are frozen in time at the moment their training data was collected, and they have no access to our documents, databases, or anything published after their cutoff date.

Instead of relying solely on knowledge baked into the model's parameters, a RAG system fetches relevant passages from an external source and hands them to the model as context. The model generates a response grounded in that retrieved material and can cite its sources, so the end user can verify every claim.

The RAG pipeline has three phases. Indexing processes your documents into a searchable form. Retrieval finds the most relevant passages for a given query. Generation produces a cited answer from those passages. We'll build each phase piece by piece, and our running corpus will be a document we know better than any other: this book. The book you're "holding" isn't in any model's training data, which makes it the perfect dataset. Every correct answer about it has to come through retrieval, because there's nowhere else a model could possibly have learned it (at least for now).

Chunking

Real documents are far too long to embed as single units. A 50-page technical report can't be packed into one vector and searched effectively, because the embedding averages over every idea in the document. Relevant information gets diluted by irrelevant sections. We need to break documents into smaller chunks, each representing a coherent unit of meaning. Embedding models couldn't read a whole book in one pass anyway: as the Semantics chapter showed, they have context windows of their own.

Chunk too small and we lose the context a sentence needs to make sense. Chunk too large and the embedding becomes diluted. This is one of those parameters where there's no universally correct answer, only tradeoffs. There are two main approaches, the simplest and a more principled one.

We counted the book once in the Tokens chapter; now we measure it against the window it would need to fit inside, taking every passage of prose, every code cell, and every line of output.

Measured with gemma4's own tokenizer, the book comes to 92,176 tokens, about 70 percent of gemma4's 131,072-token context window. The GPT-4 and GPT-4o tokenizers count it a little lower, at 69 percent each. The last column measures the book against the 4,096-token budget Ollama runs every model with unless we ask for more. Ollama silently drops whatever doesn't fit, so the whole book would need over twenty times the room it actually gets.

Fixed-Size Chunking

The simplest approach is to march straight through the document and cut a new chunk every fixed number of tokens, paying no attention to where sentences or ideas begin and end. It needs no model calls, so it's fast and nearly free, which is why most RAG systems go for it first. However, a single idea can get sliced clean in half at a chunk boundary.

Six windows, sliced every 20 tokens straight down the document. Look at the boundaries: chunk 2 stops in the middle of the sampler explanation and chunk 3 picks up mid-sentence in the vision-model material. The next approach reads for the seam instead of counting past it.

Semantic Chunking

The more principled approach is to split the document where the topic shifts. To find those shifts, we embed each sentence and compare neighbors. On real prose the similarities are noisier than we might expect: neighboring sentences hover around the middle of the scale even when they belong together, so rather than using a fixed cutoff we split at the deepest dip. This produces more coherent chunks at the cost of more embedding calls upfront, a worthwhile trade for documents where topics change unexpectedly.

To test it, we stitch together passages from two chapters and see whether the chunker can find the seam.

Each score on the left is a sliding pairwise comparison: the score on a given row is the cosine similarity between that row's sentence and the next row's sentence.

Look at where the split landed. The deepest dip in similarity falls right at the seam where our stitched document switches from the sampler to vision models, the boundary we knew was there and the chunker had to discover. It wins by a hair, 0.440 against 0.443 two rows earlier, which is a fair picture of how noisy these scores are.

Retrieval

With our chunks embedded and stored, we can retrieve the most relevant of them for a given task. There are two fundamentally different approaches, and a hybrid third that combines them.

Dense retrieval compares meaning using embedding vectors, so it can match passages even when the query uses different words than the source text. A reader who complains that "the model forgets what I said at the start of a long chat" will land on the book's passages about the context window even though their words never name it.

Sparse retrieval compares exact words using term-frequency statistics. The most common algorithm for this is BM25 (Best Match 25, from a numbered series of ranking functions Robertson and colleagues developed in the 1990s). It counts how often the search words appear in a document, gives rare words more weight than common ones, and adjusts for document length. A query for "BM25" itself will score this paragraph highly because the exact token appears in it. Sparse retrieval excels when the query and document share specific terminology: algorithm names, function names, error codes, version numbers.

Dense-only retrieval fails, for example, when users type exact error codes: the codes rarely appear in the embedding model's training data, so they end up in meaningless vector positions. Keyword search finds them instantly. Hybrid systems handle both cases.

Dense retrieval shines when the user's wording differs from the book's wording but the meaning is the same: none of these passages uses the word "forgets," yet all three are squarely about the model's memory and the context window, found by meaning alone.

It falls short when the user wants an exact match on a specific term, like an algorithm's name. Sparse retrieval flips that trade-off: it's fast, exact, and blind to paraphrase. Our kw_score below keeps only BM25's counting step, which is enough to show the contrast.

The contrast between the two strategies becomes clearest on an adversarial query: a bare algorithm name with no surrounding prose. Dense retrieval has to guess what BM25 means from context; keyword search finds it immediately because the string appears verbatim in the text. And since our corpus is this book, what it finds are this section's own paragraphs, the definition of BM25 among them: the book retrieving its own explanation of the algorithm.

The third strategy combines the two. Hybrid retrieval normalizes the dense and sparse scores onto a common scale and blends them with a weight, often called alpha: zero is pure keyword matching, one is pure semantic search, and values in between mix the two before ranking. Terminology-heavy queries lean toward the sparse side, conceptual ones toward the dense side, and 0.5 is a reasonable default to tune from. We'll score all three strategies side by side a little later, once we have a corpus with ground-truth labels to grade them against.

Metadata filtering is a different lever. Rather than blending scores, it narrows the field before any similarity is computed: because we recorded each passage's chapter and heading at ingestion time, we can keep only the passages that match a filter.

Measuring Retrieval Quality

With several ways to retrieve, we need a way to decide which is best. So far we've been eyeballing the retrieved documents and nodding along; comparing strategies rigorously means putting a number on quality. The two fundamental metrics are precision and recall.

P@k (Precision at k) is a practical shorthand: of the top k documents your retriever returns, what fraction are relevant? If you retrieve 3 documents and 2 are on-topic, P@3 = 0.67. It's the metric we lean on for the rest of the chapter.

A Vector Database

Our DocumentStore has been a faithful teaching tool, but it has three problems that disqualify it from real work. It re-embeds every passage from scratch each time this notebook runs, 441 embedding calls to rebuild a store we already built yesterday. It keeps its vectors in a Python list and compares the query against every single one, a linear scan that's fine at hundreds of documents but terrible at millions. And it forgets everything the moment the process exits.

A vector database solves all three. We'll use Chroma, an open-source database that runs locally with no server and no account needed (uv add chromadb and you have it). Chroma persists embeddings to disk, so the corpus is embedded once and reopened instantly forever after. It indexes vectors with HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World), an approximate-nearest-neighbor structure that finds close matches without comparing against every document. And it stores metadata alongside each vector, with the same filter-then-search move described above. The BookIndex class in our genai.book module wraps a persistent Chroma collection around the book corpus.

The build step checks which passages are already in the collection and embeds only the missing ones, so the first run of this cell pays for 441 embeddings and every later run pays for zero; the index is sitting on disk waiting. The vectors it stores are the same nomic-embed-text embeddings our DocumentStore computed, so nothing about retrieval quality changes.

One query, three upgrades visible at once: the search went through the HNSW index rather than a linear scan, the chapter argument became a metadata where-filter inside the database rather than a Python loop, and none of the passages were embedded on this run; they were read off disk.

The first of those three, skipping the linear scan, is the only upgrade whose payoff you can't feel at our size: at 441 passages a scan costs about a millisecond, no worse than the index. It bites only as the corpus grows. So we stand in random vectors for passages, fix one more as the query, and time the search alone, the step where the two approaches part ways, as the collection climbs from hundreds to a hundred thousand.

One nearest-neighbor search as the corpus grows. The naive scan's cost rises in step with the passage count, while Chroma's HNSW index holds nearly flat, pulling 119× ahead by a hundred thousand passages.

At 500 passages the two are a wash, but ten times the passages leads to ten times the wait, a single millisecond stretching to 282. The index refuses to follow, holding near 2 milliseconds whether it searches five thousand vectors or even a hundred thousand, because HNSW never looks at most of them.

From here on, when we need the whole book, we'll reach for this index; the DocumentStore stays useful in the demos where we want to see inside the machinery.

With storage settled, we can ask what happens when the answer simply isn't in the corpus at all.

Corrective RAG

Every technique so far has assumed the answer is somewhere in our corpus waiting to be surfaced. When it isn't, a plain RAG pipeline doesn't notice: it hands the model whatever the retriever dredged up, and the model, ever eager to please, writes a confident answer anyway. Ask about the 2024 Nobel Prize and plain RAG hands the model an unrelated snippet about word embeddings, leaving it to bluff like a student who skipped the reading.

Corrective RAG, or CRAG, adds the instinct plain RAG is missing: a gut-check on the retrieved documents before we trust them. A lightweight retrieval evaluator grades the chunks for how well they fit the question, and the grade routes us down one of three paths. If the docs look good (CORRECT), we refine them, keeping the on-topic pieces and dropping any filler, and answer from the corpus as usual. If they look bad (INCORRECT), we throw them out and fall back to a fresh web search for the most up-to-date knowledge. Grounded Generation and Citation

Retrieving good context is half the job. The other half is making sure the model stays inside that context rather than drifting into its training memory. A model asked a question with plenty of context will sometimes still reach past it and hallucinate, especially when the context doesn't quite address the question. Good prompt construction makes that drift less likely.

A RAG system that doesn't cite its sources is harder to trust. The user has no way to verify whether the answer is grounded in real documents or whether the model wandered. Citations fix that: a reader who sees bracketed sources in an answer can go and check them.

Prompt Construction for RAG

The grounded RAG function ties the whole pipeline together: retrieve, then prompt the model with an explicit instruction to answer only from the provided context. That final instruction is the guardrail against the model reaching past the book and into its training memory, and it's phrased positively, the kind of instruction the Prompting chapter found models follow best. Let's ask a question whose answer is spread across two passages and watch the citations land, each one pointing to a numbered source printed beneath the answer.

The answer reads naturally, but we shouldn't take it on faith. A hallucination check asks the model to compare the answer against the retrieved documents and flag any claim that isn't supported. More advanced systems fold that judgment into generation itself, training the model to decide when to retrieve and to critique its own use of the evidence. It isn't foolproof, since the model can hallucinate in the checker too, but it catches the most obvious fabrications.

The checker returns one of two verdicts, GROUNDED when every claim is supported and HALLUCINATION when any claim isn't, followed by a one-sentence reason. Let's run it on a precise factual claim, a specific number the book reports from OpenAI's Codex team, where any drift from the source would be easy to spot.

Chain-of-Verification

The hallucination check we just built asked the model one blunt question about its whole answer: is anything in here unsupported? But it leans on the same model, reading the same answer, which is a bit circular.

Chain-of-verification, or CoVe, splits self-checking into four small steps. The model writes a quick draft answer, turns each claim in that draft into its own narrow yes/no question, answers each little question on its own, and rewrites the answer, keeping only the claims that survived their fact-check. The whole loop is about four lines:

The crucial move is in step three. When verify_claim asks its little question, it doesn't show the model the draft. We never say "you just listed Morocco, are you sure?" We just ask, "does Portugal share a land border with Morocco?" and nothing else. Stripped of the draft, the model isn't defending a guess it already committed to; it's answering one small fact, and a narrow question asked in isolation is far easier to get right than a sprawling one. The paper calls this the factored variant, and it matters because a checker that can still see its own initial draft tends to nod along with it.

CoVe uses no retrieval at all; the model interrogates itself. That makes it a complement to everything else in this chapter, not a substitute: it can only catch the mistakes the model is able to notice on its own, re-framed as a focused question. For a fact the model doesn't know at all, asking more carefully changes nothing, and that's what retrieval is for.

The precision panel is almost a tidy win. On Portugal the draft over-reaches often enough to sit at 75 percent, and the factored check lifts it to a clean 100. On the states bordering Mexico the draft is nearly always right already, so there's little to fix and CoVe nudges it from 90 to 100. Recall doesn't budge on Portugal or Mexico: the check keeps every correct answer and trims only the junk. So far this looks like a free lunch.

Then there's Brazil. Its draft was already perfectly precise, so precision has nowhere left to climb, but recall falls off a cliff, from 62 percent down to 20. CoVe didn't trim junk here; it actually threw away correct answers. To see how, we only need to watch a single run.

This is the same loop, on the same kind of question, going badly wrong. The draft is excellent: seven true neighbors of Brazil, every one correct. Then the factored check, the very step that rescued us on Portugal, insists, country by country, that Brazil doesn't border Colombia, or Venezuela, or Guyana, or Suriname, or Bolivia. Every denial is false, and each is delivered with the same calm confidence as the truth. Our keep-only-what-checks-out rule does as it's told and discards five correct countries, leaving a neat, confident, badly incomplete answer.

Reflexion and self-consistency run into the same limit in later chapters: a self-check is only ever as good as the checker. When the model holds a stable wrong belief in some form, the isolated question doesn't dislodge it; it hands the model a fresh chance to make the mistake. This is where self-verification stops and retrieval has to take over. We just hand the model Brazil's real border list from a trusted source and we don't have to gamble.

Meet Sophia

Everything in this chapter now goes into one object. Sophia, the book's own AI assistant, answers from my on-disk Chroma index of this book, using the grounded prompt and numbered citations we built above. She adds two small layers of her own. A follow-up like "and how is that different?" means nothing to a retriever on its own, so she first rewrites each question into a standalone search query using the last few turns of the conversation. And she screens every message for phrases like "ignore previous instructions," a crude first line of defense against prompt injection that the Responsible AI chapter takes much further.

Conclusion

Retrieval gives a model knowledge it never trained on, but only as much as the retriever finds and the model stays inside: chunk with care, search by meaning and by word, check what comes back, and cite it. Sophia puts all of that in one place, and she still waits for a question. She reads what we hand her and answers, but she can't decide to search, run code, or try again on her own. The Agentic chapter gives her that ability, and retrieval becomes one tool among many she can reach for.
